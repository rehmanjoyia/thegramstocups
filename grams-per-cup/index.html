Grams per Cup: Ingredient Weight Chart

One cup does not have a single weight in grams: our baseline reference is 120g for all-purpose flour, 198g for granulated sugar and 227g for butter.

Find your ingredient below, including the preparation and source used for its conversion.

Grams per cup by ingredient

These are the baseline weights used for this site's US Customary cup setting. They are reference values, not a promise that every cup you fill will weigh exactly the same.

Reference grams per US Customary cup
Ingredient Grams per cup Ingredient or preparation covered Source
All-purpose flour 120g Fluffed, spooned and leveled King Arthur
Granulated white sugar 198g Dry granulated sugar King Arthur
Butter 227g Standard butter, not whipped Land O'Lakes
Brown sugar 213g Packed, light or dark King Arthur
Powdered sugar 113g Unsifted confectioners' sugar King Arthur
Rolled oats 89g Dry old-fashioned oats; see oat note below King Arthur
White rice 185g Raw, regular long-grain white rice USDA, p. 50
Honey 339g Strained or extracted honey USDA, p. 74
Olive oil 216g Olive oil; not every cooking oil USDA, p. 24
Whole milk 244g Fluid whole milk; source entry is 3.3% fat USDA, p. 20

The USDA links open the relevant pages of Nutritive Value of Foods, revised in 2002. These are published household-measure references, not recent laboratory tests of your ingredients. The source's printed page numbers differ from the PDF viewer's page count.

Match the ingredient before using its weight

Flour and sugars: Preparation changes what fits inside a cup. Keep the flour measurement loose and level, pack brown sugar when the reference calls for it, and use the powdered-sugar figure for unsifted sugar. Each ingredient link above gives more detail.

Oats: The 89g reference comes from King Arthur's generic “old-fashioned or quick-cooking” entry. Its separately named King Arthur Rolled Oats product is listed at 113g per cup. Our default is the generic dry-oat reference; it is not a claim that every brand or oat type weighs 89g.

Rice: Choose the uncooked reference only for dry rice. Cooking changes the grain and its water content, so the same number should not be used for a cup of cooked rice.

Oil and milk: The table names the specific products used. Olive oil is not a universal reference for every oil, and whole milk is not a reference for condensed milk, milk powder or plant-based drinks.

How to use grams per cup

To convert cups into weight, multiply by the ingredient's reference:

Grams = cups × grams per cup

For example, 1½ cups of flour gives 1.5 × 120 = 180g. Use our cups-to-grams converter for other quantities.

To convert a weight into cups, divide instead:

Cups = grams ÷ grams per cup

For example, 100g of granulated sugar gives 100 ÷ 198 = about 0.51 cups. Use the grams-to-cups converter to choose an ingredient and amount.

For a reference built around common recipe quantities, use our printable conversion chart.

Does cup size change these weights?

Yes. This table gives our US Customary baselines. The converter uses 236.588mL for that setting, 240mL for US Legal and 250mL for Metric.

We assign the published cup weights to the site's US Customary baseline, then scale them in proportion to the selected cup volume. That assignment is our convention; a source's culinary cup entry does not establish a density measured at precisely 236.588mL.

For example, the estimated metric-cup weight for flour is 120 × 250 ÷ 236.588 = about 126.8g. Keep this calculated adjustment separate from the source's published 120g-per-cup entry. Read the cup-size guide and conversion methodology for the standards and calculation rules.

What if my recipe gives a different weight?

Follow the recipe's own gram equivalent when it provides one. A different reference may reflect the recipe developer's product, measuring method or rounding convention.

Avoid averaging two references or switching between them partway through a recipe. Choose the entry that matches your ingredient and use it consistently. If you already have a weight and a kitchen scale, you can weigh that amount directly.

Questions about ingredient weights

Is grams per cup the same as density?

It describes the weight of a specified cup of an ingredient. Density is often expressed in grams per milliliter instead. To derive grams per milliliter from a cup reference, divide its weight by the assumed cup volume. The result still inherits the reference's preparation and measurement assumptions.

Why can flour and powdered sugar have similar cup weights?

Similar weights per cup do not make ingredients interchangeable. The figures describe how much fits in the chosen volume, not how the ingredients behave in a recipe. Use the ingredient requested by the recipe.
